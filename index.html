What it looks for

Scams change their wording, but they rely on the same few levers: pressure, fear, greed, trust and secrecy, plus a way to get your money, codes or details. Second Look looks for those levers and for links that lie about where they go.

    How well it works, measured honestly

    We wrote labelled test messages (all synthetic) and measured the rules in rounds. Each round, a brand-new set of messages was scored once, before we looked at what it missed. The first round showed the rules were overfit to our own examples (only 36% of new scams caught), so we rebuilt them around the structure of scams rather than specific phrases.

    The number we stand behind is the last round, scored once and never tuned: it catches about three in four new scams from the words alone, and wrongly flagged one of the 12 ordinary messages. Your answers to the three quick questions add what the words can't show.

    What it can't do

    • It can't tell you a message is safe. "No warning signs found" only means none of the common tricks appeared.
    • It can't see who really sent a message, check phone numbers, or visit links. It reads the words only.
    • It misses scams that start innocently, like a friendly "wrong number" chat that turns to money weeks later.
    • It's English-only for now. Reporting help covers the US, UK, India, Canada and Australia, with general advice elsewhere.
    • It reads text and QR codes, not screenshots or voice calls (you can type what a caller said).
    • QR reading uses your browser's built-in scanner, which Chrome and Edge have on Android, Mac and ChromeOS. Elsewhere you can type the address your camera shows.
